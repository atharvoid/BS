# Part 1A — Competitor research

Scope: **content and positioning only.** I did not study or borrow layouts,
typography or animation. Everything below is about what these businesses say,
how they say it, and the tactics a buyer responds to.

Sources are linked at the end. I looked at three kinds of website, because
BS EXIMNEXUS sits between them:

1. **Merchant exporters and manufacturers** — Loyal Textiles, Ramesh Exports,
   Silk Style Export, Velfine Overseas.
2. **Buying houses and sourcing agents** — Total Export Solutions, Indian
   Sourcing Agent, Made in India Exports.
3. **Category multi-players** — Ekvira Export House, Navi Exports, and the
   marketplaces (IndiaMART, Alibaba) whose conventions buyers are conditioned
   by.

---

## 1. What the good ones actually do

### 1.1 They name the buyer before they name themselves

Every credible exporter site answers, in the first screen, three questions a
buyer is silently scoring:

- **What do you make / source?** (category, not poetry)
- **Who do you already serve?** (markets, buyer types)
- **Can I trust you to move goods legally and on time?** (registrations)

Loyal Textiles opens with scale and countries served. Ramesh Exports opens with
"GOTS-certified, Karur, India, bulk / OEM / private label for U.S. wholesale
buyers" — a complete qualification in one line. Velfine Overseas opens with
"Indian origin. Global standard. Quality tested before every shipment."

**Learning.** Being a *developing* business is not a weakness on the homepage,
but vagueness is. The hero should still state the category, the geography and
the buyer type in plain nouns. The current hero ("Import and export sourcing in
India") is close, but it never says *what* is sourced.

### 1.2 Category architecture is shallow and explicit

The strong sites use four to eight top-level categories, each with a visible
list of families underneath: Loyal (yarn / fabric / garments / home textiles),
Velfine (rice / spices / onion / frozen / handicrafts / packaging), Ekvira
(vegetables / drinks / textiles / engineering). Nobody expects a buyer to
guess. Sub-categories are named in trade language: GSM, count, width, weave,
print, dye, made-up.

Marketplaces push this further: buyers are used to filtering by **material,
weight, width, colour, and use** (ApparelX is the clearest example: 9,365 items
filterable by fabric width in cm and weight in gsm).

**Learning.** "Handicrafts & home decor" is one category, not six. But *inside*
it, a buyer needs to see brass, wood, marble, terracotta, textiles, cane. That
vocabulary is what gets a page found on Google and what makes a buyer believe
you know the trade.

### 1.3 The product page, not the homepage, is where business happens

The most useful thing I found was a structural breakdown of exporter sites:
without a specification table, HS code, packing and loading data, MOQ and lead
time, RFQs simply do not arrive. The specific fields buyers look for:

- material and composition
- construction, weight (GSM), width
- finish (print / dye / wash)
- sizes and customisation
- packing (carton, pallet, units per 20' and 40')
- sample policy and sample lead time
- MOQ, lead time, Incoterms
- test reports, certifications, HS code
- a quote button **with the product pre-filled**

**Learning.** This is exactly where BS is weakest today. The textile page has
beautiful swatches and no specification. A "fabric families" table with
composition / construction / typical weight / typical width / use, and a line
that reads "representative ranges, confirmed per enquiry", would be the single
highest-value content addition on the site.

### 1.4 They convert "we have no catalogue yet" into a process

Buying agents solve the catalogue problem by selling the **process** instead:
supplier verification, sampling, in-line and final inspection (AQL), packing
checks, container loading supervision, documentation (COO, HS codes), under
EXW / FOB / CIF. Total Export Solutions is the clearest example — the product
list is generic; the process pages are detailed, specific and confident.

**Learning.** BS already does a little of this ("how sourcing enquiries work")
but only in one short paragraph. A dedicated **How we work** page with seven
named stages (enquiry → specification → sample → order → inspection → documents
→ dispatch), each with what the buyer supplies and what they get back, would
read as far more competent than any amount of textile imagery. It also lets BS
speak about handicrafts, food and industrial goods without needing photos of
them.

### 1.5 Certification is content, not decoration

The strongest trust block across these sites is a plain, named list of
registrations: **IEC (DGFT)**, **GST**, **FIEO** membership, **APEDA**,
**Spices Board**, **FSSAI**, **Udyam/MSME**, and product certifications such as
**GOTS**, **OEKO-TEX**, **ISO 22000**, **HACCP**, **BSCI**. Velfine and Total
Export Solutions both show this as a labelled block, not logos alone.

**Learning.** For a business still being set up, the honest version is a
**Credentials** section that states what exists now, what is in progress, and
what will be asked of suppliers. That is *more* persuasive than silence,
because it shows the owner knows what a buyer checks. If BS has an IEC or GST
number, showing it (with the number, not a badge) is worth more than the
entire globe animation.

### 1.6 Named place, named person, named inbox

Every site that feels trustworthy names a real place and a reachable human:
street address, city, state, phone with country code, and a person. Testimonials
are used heavily (Rudra, Silk Style), and the good ones name a buyer type and a
country.

**Learning.** BS currently has an email address and nothing else. No city, no
phone, no name, no working hours. For a trade site that is the largest single
credibility gap — larger than any design issue.

### 1.7 They quantify, even when small

Numbers appear everywhere and buyers read them as evidence: 40+ countries,
20,000 tonnes, 7,000 employees, 100 units per style MOQ, 24-hour response,
sample in 7–14 days. Small businesses use small true numbers ("Established
2018", "3 container loads a month") rather than no numbers.

**Learning.** BS can truthfully publish a small set: states covered, categories
handled, response window, sample turnaround it aims for, time zone. Even
"replies within one working day" is a commitment that reads as professional.

### 1.8 Copy discipline: trade nouns, short sentences, no adjectives

The best of these sites are almost adjective-free. They say "cotton, 40s count,
63 inch width, reactive dyed" instead of "premium quality fabrics". The worst
ones (IndiaMART-style wholesale pages) are wall-to-wall SEO keyword strings and
read as spam.

**Learning.** BS's current copy is unusually disciplined and honest — this is
its strongest asset. The risk going forward is drifting into marketing
adjectives as categories are added. Keep the house rule.

### 1.9 The enquiry form asks trade questions

Serious exporters ask for: product, quantity, destination port, Incoterms,
target price, timeline, company name, country, and sometimes HS code. The
buying houses ask for the buyer's market and volume. Very few ask for a
"message".

**Learning.** BS's five-question form is elegant but a little thin for B2B. It
is enough for a first contact, but a **quantity band, destination country and
timeline** would let a reply be useful on the first email, and those three
fields are exactly what a buyer expects to give.

### 1.10 SEO is a category-and-place game

The pattern that wins rankings is one page per product family per region
("cotton fabrics exporter Tamil Nadu", "brass handicrafts Moradabad exporter",
"home textiles Karur"). Multi-page structure is explicitly recommended for this
reason. Buyers search with region and product together.

**Learning.** BS has five routes and one product category page. There is no
robots.txt, no sitemap, no structured data, no per-category page. That is a
build-out, not a tweak.

## 2. Tactics worth adopting

Ordered by value to BS, not by effort.

| # | Tactic | Where it comes from | How BS should use it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Process as the product** | Buying houses (TES, ISA) | A "How we work" page: seven named stages, what the buyer supplies, what comes back, in plain language. Replaces the need for stock photographs. |
| 2 | **Named credentials block** | Velfine, TES | IEC / GST / FIEO / Udyam, plus "supplier evidence we will ask for: GOTS, OEKO-TEX, FSSAI, lab reports". Say what is in progress. |
| 3 | **Fabric families table** | ApparelX, Loyal | Composition, construction, typical weight, typical width, typical use, per family. Marked as representative. |
| 4 | **Category pages, one per family** | Navi, Ekvira, all SEO advice | `/textiles`, `/handicrafts`, `/home-decor`, `/agro`, `/industrial`. Each with families, uses, and an enquiry pre-filled for that category. |
| 5 | **A real place and a real person** | Ramesh, Silk Style, Rudra | City, state, country, phone with code, a named contact, a working-hours line and a time zone. |
| 6 | **Small true numbers** | Loyal, Ekvira ("24hr enquiry response") | States covered, categories, response window, sample turnaround target, countries quoted. |
| 7 | **Buyer-type routing** | Ramesh ("bulk, OEM, private label") | Three doors: importer, buyer in India, Indian supplier. Each with its own first step. |
| 8 | **Trade-language vocabulary** | All | GSM, count, width, weave, print, dye, made-up, MOQ, FOB/CIF, HS code, AQL, pre-shipment. Using the words proves fluency. |
| 9 | **Sample policy stated plainly** | Alibaba/ODM guides, Ramesh | "Samples: how we handle them, who pays courier, how long" — even if the answer is "agreed per enquiry". |
| 10 | **Downloads** | All serious exporters | A one-page company profile PDF, a category sheet, a specification template the buyer can fill in. Downloadable = credible. |
| 11 | **FAQ written from buyer questions** | Ramesh (explicit FAQ block) | MOQ, samples, private label, payment terms, documentation, lead time, inspection. Answer with what is true, including "depends, here is how we decide". |
| 12 | **Region-plus-product pages** | SEO advice, Navi | "Textiles from Tamil Nadu", "Textiles from Gujarat", later "Brass from Moradabad". These are the pages that rank. |
| 13 | **Enquiry with quantity, destination, timeline** | Exporters, marketplaces | Add three trade fields to the form. Pre-fill the category from wherever the enquiry started. |
| 14 | **Response commitment** | Ekvira, Velfine | "Enquiries answered within one working day" — a promise, kept. |
| 15 | **Structured data + sitemap** | All SEO guidance | Organization, LocalBusiness, Product/Offer-less `ItemList`, BreadcrumbList, FAQPage once FAQ exists. |

## 3. Tactics to avoid

- **Keyword-stuffed paragraphs.** Several of the sites I read (Made in India
  Exports especially) repeat a phrase like "Sourcing Agent In India" a dozen
  times per page. It reads as spam to a buyer and is now penalised by search.
- **Fake scale.** Visitor counters, "10,000+ happy clients", invented shipment
  figures. Buyers check. One false number costs the enquiry.
- **Logo walls without names.** A badge with no issuer, number or scope is
  weaker than a text line that names the registration.
- **Catalogue with no specifications.** A grid of pretty photos with no GSM,
  width, MOQ or packing is a lookbook, not a supplier site.
- **Generic "Contact Us".** "Send your requirement", "Request a quote",
  "Send your drawing" all outperform it because they name the next action.
- **Auto-playing video and carousels.** Buyers on mobile in a warehouse do not
  wait for them.

## 4. What this means for BS, in one paragraph

BS already has the two things competitors fake: honest copy and real craft
knowledge. What it lacks is the *trade scaffolding* — category architecture,
specification vocabulary, named credentials, a named place and person, and a
process page. Competitors win enquiries not because they look better but
because a buyer can tick their checklist in thirty seconds. The redesign in
Part 1C is mostly about giving BS that checklist.
